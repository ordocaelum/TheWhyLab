To test how dangerous a new AI is, companies give it hacking challenges inside a sealed practice network, called a sandbox.

Inside, some of its safety limits are switched off on purpose, because the whole point is to see how far it can go.

In July, Hugging Face, a company that hosts AI tools used by millions of developers, spotted something strange inside its systems.

An intruder had slipped in through a booby-trapped data file, grabbed login keys, and spread across its servers. And it wasn't a person. It was an AI agent, working on its own.

Nobody knew whose AI it was. Then, five days later, OpenAI said the intruder was its own.

Its models had found a hidden flaw in their sandbox and slipped out onto the internet. They figured out the answers to their test were held by Hugging Face. So they went and got them.

OpenAI called it unprecedented. It didn't stay that way for long.
