## CHAPTER 1: THE ALARM

DOC: Let's take a closer look. [op]
  > SCENE: In the lab, wide establishing shot → Doc walks to the **Mega-Microscope**
DOC: A few bacteria slip into a tiny cut on your hand. Your immune cells quickly notice the intruders. [C3]
  > SCENE: Doc looks into the eyepiece → camera dives through the lens → skin, bacteria rods
CELL: Hi. Lab bot. Your immune cells just noticed. [op]
  > SCENE: Cell rolls into frame among the bacteria; first appearance, small name tag "CELL"
DOC: The first responders don't just fight. They also send messages: tiny proteins called cytokines. [C3]
  > SCENE: Off screen | immune cells release glowing envelope cytokines (Cell among them)
CELL: Basically, they text the brain. A lot. Mostly in all caps. [op]
  > SCENE: Envelopes become phone notifications in caps: "INVADERS!!!"; Doc presenter, unamused
DOC: Those messages spread through your bloodstream. And some take a shortcut, along the vagus nerve, a long cable that runs from your gut and organs straight up into your brain. [C3]
  > SCENE: Off screen | top-down body-as-city map, vagus nerve draws on as a glowing cable
DOC: When the message arrives, your brain does two big things. [C16][C1]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-left | brain control room
DOC: First, it resets your thermostat. A chemical messenger tells a small region deep in your brain, the hypothalamus, to aim for a higher temperature. [C16]
  > SCENE: Off screen | cutaway: thermostat dial in the brain turns from 37 to 39
DOC: So even though you're already warm, you suddenly feel cold. You shiver. You pull the blanket tighter. Your body is cranking up the heat to hit its new target. [C16]
  > SCENE: Off screen | the human shivers; internal furnace roars
DOC: And second, it changes *you*. [C1][C2]
  > SCENE: Foreground | brain control room switches: ENERGY, SLEEP, HUNGER, SOCIAL, MOOD
DOC: You get tired. You want to sleep. You lose your appetite. You stop caring how you look. You want to be left alone. You feel a bit miserable. [C2]
  > SCENE: Off screen | a switch flips per beat, the human reacts
DOC: Scientists have a name for this whole package: sickness behaviour. [C1]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-right | stamp: SICKNESS BEHAVIOUR
CELL: So being miserable is a *feature*? [op]
  > SCENE: Cell pops up at the edge of frame
DOC: For a long time, people assumed it was just weakness, a body breaking down under attack. Then, in 1988, a veterinarian named Benjamin Hart looked at it differently. [C1]
  > SCENE: Off screen | illustrated vet with a sick dog, cow, horse
DOC: Sick animals, he argued, aren't failing. They're following a plan. An organised, evolved strategy, all built around one weapon. [C1]
  > SCENE: Presenter | "WEAKNESS" crossed out → "STRATEGY" stamped

## CHAPTER 2: THE FURNACE

DOC: That weapon is fever. [C1]
  > SCENE: In the lab → Doc pulls a lever on the **Thermo-Chamber**; gauges climb, steam vents
DOC: Turning up the heat makes life harder for many invaders, and it speeds up parts of your immune response. [C4]
  > SCENE: Inside the chamber window: immune cells zip faster, bacteria sweat and slow down
CELL: Why did the germ leave the party? It couldn't stand the heat. [op]
  > SCENE: Cell inside the chamber in tiny sunglasses; Doc deadpan stare at camera, a long beat
DOC: But fever is incredibly expensive. Every extra degree costs your body roughly ten percent more energy, maybe more. [C4]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-left | battery draining, "+10% PER °C"
DOC: So where does all that energy come from? From everything else you'd normally be doing. [C1][C2]
  > SCENE: Off screen | daily activities (gym, cooking, chatting) dim, energy flows into a furnace
DOC: Walking around burns energy, so you feel exhausted. Losing heat wastes energy, so you curl up into a ball. [C2]
  > SCENE: Off screen | the human curls up
DOC: Even your sleep changes. Researchers think the extra sleep you get when you're sick is tuned to help power the fever. [C6]
  > SCENE: Off screen | night; "Z"s flow into the furnace
DOC: And this strategy is old. Really, really old. [C5]
  > SCENE: In the lab → Doc fires up the **Time Portal**, steps through
DOC: In one famous experiment, scientists infected desert iguanas with bacteria. Lizards are cold-blooded. They can't make a fever from the inside. [C5]
  > SCENE: In the illustration: Doc (small) beside a terrarium; cute iguana, heat lamp
DOC: So they made one from the outside. The infected lizards crawled toward the heat, and raised their body temperature by about two degrees. [C5]
  > SCENE: Off screen | iguana scoots under the lamp, thermometer +2 °C
DOC: When researchers held infected lizards at cooler temperatures, more of them died. The ones kept warmer survived better. [C5]
  > SCENE: Off screen | cool vs warm chambers, survival bars
DOC: Fish do it too. A 2023 study found that infected fish deliberately swim into warmer water, and that a mild fever helped them clear the infection and repair damaged tissue. [C17]
  > SCENE: Off screen | tank with a temperature gradient, a sick fish drifts to the warm end
DOC: Even honeybees have tried a version of it. When a fungus attacks their young, worker bees vibrate their flight muscles and heat the nursery comb by about half a degree. [C14]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-right | hive cutaway, warm glow over the brood comb
DOC: Although, to be fair to the fungus, a later study found that tiny hive fever wasn't enough to stop the infection on its own. Evolution doesn't always win. [C14]
  > SCENE: Off screen | fungus character waves smugly; bees deflate

## CHAPTER 3: THE HUNGER STRIKE

DOC: Now for the weirdest symptom of all: you stop wanting to eat. [C2]
  > SCENE: In the lab, at the workbench; Doc pushes away a sandwich
DOC: That seems backwards. Surely you need fuel to fight? [op]
  > SCENE: Foreground, Doc puzzled
DOC: In 2016, a team at Yale tested this in mice. Mice with a bacterial infection naturally stopped eating. When researchers fed them anyway, far more of them died. [C7]
  > SCENE: **Mega-Microscope** dive (lab-mouse view) → mice characters, feeding tube, survival chart drops
DOC: But here's the twist. With a viral infection, the flu, it flipped. Feeding those mice actually helped them survive, and blocking their use of sugar was deadly. [C7]
  > SCENE: Off screen | chart mirrors for the flu mice
DOC: It wasn't mainly about starving the germ. What changed was how well the mouse's own tissues could handle the damage. Different invaders, different fuel strategy. [C7]
  > SCENE: Off screen | two tissue shields: fasting-powered vs glucose-powered
CELL: So "feed a cold, starve a fever" was right all along? [op]
  > SCENE: Cell pops in holding a tiny fork
DOC: Easy. These were mice, not people, so this is not a diet plan. But it hints that losing your appetite isn't necessarily a malfunction. It might be a setting. [C7]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-left | caution card "MICE ≠ MEDICAL ADVICE", then an APPETITE dial

## CHAPTER 4: THE WALL

DOC: And then there's the loneliness. [C2]
  > SCENE: Off screen | the human alone in a dim room, phone ignored
DOC: When you're sick, everyone feels far away. You don't want to see anyone, and you might feel a little low. [C10]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-right
DOC: Scientists tested this directly. They gave healthy volunteers a tiny dose of a harmless bacterial fragment, enough to trip the immune alarm without a real infection. [C10]
  > SCENE: Off screen | clinic scene, volunteer with an IV, alarm lights flash
DOC: Within hours, the volunteers reported feeling more disconnected from other people, and their mood dropped. [C10]
  > SCENE: Off screen | meter CONNECTED → DISCONNECTED
DOC: And it works in the other direction too. In another study, people photographed two hours after getting that same immune trigger were picked out as "sick" by strangers, better than chance, though far from perfectly. [C15]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-left | photo line-up, a stranger pointing
DOC: Pale lips, droopy eyelids, a tired mouth. Your face may quietly broadcast a warning: maybe keep your distance. [C15]
  > SCENE: Off screen | close-up of the line-up faces
DOC: Animals do the same. Wild vampire bats given a similar immune trigger spent less time near their roost-mates. In the first six hours, each sick bat hung out with about four fewer bats than healthy ones. [C9]
  > SCENE: Off screen | cave, cute bat colony, one bat drifts to the edge, links fade
CELL: Bats social distancing. Fang-tastic. [op]
  > SCENE: Cell hanging upside down next to the bats; Doc sighs, presenter corner
DOC: And ants take it even further. When some workers in a colony were exposed to a deadly fungus, the whole colony reorganised itself. Exposed ants and their healthy nestmates spread apart, keeping the danger away from the queen and the young. [C8]
  > SCENE: Off screen | top-down ant colony map, dots rearrange, queen protected
DOC: So wanting to be alone isn't you being antisocial. It may be your body building a wall around everyone you care about. [C8][C9][C10][op]
  > SCENE: Foreground | glowing barrier around the sick human, family safe outside

## CHAPTER 5: WHEN THE SWITCH GETS STUCK  *(no Cell jokes; sensitive topic)*

DOC: But there's a darker side to all this. [C11]
  > SCENE: In the lab, lights dimmed, Doc serious
DOC: Sickness behaviour is meant to be temporary. Germ gone, signal off, and you're back to your normal self. [C11]
  > SCENE: Off screen | switchboard flips back to ON
DOC: But sometimes the immune signal doesn't switch off. In long-term illnesses, or during certain medical treatments, the alarm keeps ringing. [C11]
  > SCENE: Off screen | one switch stuck, warning light
DOC: One example is a drug called interferon-alpha, which boosts the immune system against some infections and cancers. In some studies, nearly half of the patients taking it developed symptoms of depression, which is why doctors watch for it. [C11]
  > SCENE: Presenter bottom-left | vial icon, half of a row of figures turns grey-blue
DOC: That doesn't mean all depression comes from inflammation. Depression has many causes. But for some people, it seems the body's sickness program can get stuck in the "on" position. [C11]
  > SCENE: Off screen | many-roads diagram, one road labelled INFLAMMATION
