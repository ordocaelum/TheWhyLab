# Research: Why the Moon Looks Huge (It Isn't)

Slug: `moon-illusion` · Format: long-form, ~8 min · Thumbnail: **MOON ILLUSION**
Hook: Hunter's Moon, full 12:11 a.m. EDT Mon Oct 26 2026 (rises nearly full Sun Oct 25 and Mon Oct 26), followed by four supermoons in a row (Nov 24 → Feb).

## Brief
- **Question:** Why does the moon look gigantic when it rises?
- **Turn:** It doesn't get bigger at all (it's even slightly *farther* away). Your brain is mis-measuring it, and after 2,000+ years scientists still argue about exactly how.
- **Takeaway:** "Hold a pea at arm's length next time. The 'giant' moon is a brain bug."
- **Score:** stakes 3, surprise 5, visualisable 5, evidence 4, demand 5 (live event), not owned 3 = **25/30** ✅

## C1: The moon's angular size barely changes from horizon to overhead (~0.5°)
- Status: ✅
- Script: "the moon takes up the same tiny slice of sky, about half a degree"
- Sources: [T1] NASA APOD 2002-01-30: "The angular diameter of the Moon is always about 0.5 degrees." · [T1] NASA, "Why Does the Moon Look Larger at the Horizon? We Asked a NASA Scientist: Episode 50" (2025) · [T3 lead] Wikipedia "Moon illusion": theodolite and photographs confirm constancy.
- Nuance: tiny real changes (C2, C18). Photos taken hours apart show the same disc.

## C2: The horizon moon is ~1.5% *farther* from you than when overhead
- Status: ✅
- Script: "about one and a half percent farther away, so actually a hair smaller"
- Sources: [T1] NASA Ep. 50 (2025): moon is roughly 1.5% farther on the horizon. · [T2] Weather Network article using a NASA SVS visualisation of moonrise vs midnight distance.
- Why: overhead, you're one Earth-radius closer to it.

## C3: How big is the illusion? Usually ~50% bigger in diameter; can seem nearly twice
- Status: ✅ (range)
- Script: "people often judge it as half again as big, sometimes nearly double"
- Sources: [T1] Ross & Plug, *The Mystery of the Moon Illusion* (Oxford UP, 2002): typically ~50% apparent increase in diameter. · [T1] Kaufman & Kaufman, PNAS 97(1):500–505 (2000): horizon moon "may appear to be nearly twice the diameter of the elevated moon."

## C4: The air is not magnifying it. Refraction actually squashes the low moon slightly
- Status: ✅
- Script: "The air isn't a magnifying glass. If anything, it squashes the low moon a little flatter."
- Sources: [T1] NASA Ep. 50 (2025). · [T1] Ross & Plug (2002) on refraction accounts being wrong.

## C5: The orange colour near the horizon is real (light crosses more air; blue scattered out)
- Status: ⚠️ (physics standard; need a T1 page) → script hedges nothing numeric
- Script: "The colour is real: low moonlight crosses much more air, which scatters away the blue and leaves the orange, the same reason sunsets are red."
- Sources: [T2] multiple explainers (Rayleigh scattering; longer atmospheric path) · fact-check to confirm with NASA Space Place / NOAA.

## C6: Known for 2,000+ years. Aristotle mentions it (c. 330 BC); Ptolemy blamed refraction (Almagest); Cleomedes (c. 200 AD) and Ibn al-Haytham (Book of Optics, 1011–1022) pointed to apparent distance
- Status: ✅ (dates approximate; Ptolemy's *Optics* reading disputed)
- Script: "Aristotle wrote about it. Ptolemy blamed the air. About a thousand years ago, the scholar Ibn al-Haytham argued it was your judgement of distance."
- Sources: [T1] Ross & Plug (2002), historical chapters · [T1] Ross & Ross, *Perception* 5(4):377–85 (1976), on Ptolemy · [T1] Wade, *A Natural History of Vision* (1998) p.377ff on Aristotle · [T2] Smith (Univ. of Missouri) on Ibn al-Haytham's analysis; MDPI *Vision* 3:39 (2019).
- Nuance: al-Haytham kept refraction too and added intervening-objects/apparent-distance; don't over-credit "modern".

## C7: Ibn al-Haytham's "flattened dome": objects along the ground make the horizon sky feel farther away than the sky overhead
- Status: ✅ (as a historical idea; modern support contested)
- Sources: [T1] Ross & Plug (2002) · [T2] MDPI *Vision* (2019) · [T2] arXiv 2306.13656 "The Celestial Vault is Not a Dome" (counterpoint).

## C8: Scientists still don't agree
- Status: ✅

## C9: Kaufman & Rock (1962): hide the terrain and the illusion mostly disappears; eye elevation, colour, brightness had little effect
- Status: ✅
- Script: "When they blocked the view of the landscape, the giant moon shrank back."
- Sources: [T1] Kaufman & Rock, *Science* 136 (1962) (as summarised in Kaufman & Kaufman 2000 and Ross & Plug 2002) · [T2] Scientific American, "Why Does the Moon Look Bigger Near the Horizon?"

## C10: Kaufman & Kaufman (2000): with artificial moons, people placed the horizon moon ~4.2× farther away (individuals 1.6–6.5×)
- Status: ✅
- Script: "people set the horizon moon about four times farther away than the high one"
- Sources: [T1] Kaufman & Kaufman, PNAS (2000), "approximately 4.2 times farther away"; range 1.58–6.48 · [T2] SpaceNews summary (Jan 2000).

## C11: The paradox: asked directly, most people say the big horizon moon looks *closer*
- Status: ✅
- Script: "ask people and most will say the giant moon looks closer"
- Sources: [T1] Kaufman & Kaufman PNAS (2000) describe this size–distance paradox · [T1] Restle, *Science* 167:1092 (1970); Boring (1962) (via Wikipedia's citation list: "about 90%").
- Nuance: don't quote 90% (secondary); say "most".

## C12: The Ponzo illusion: the same size object looks bigger when cues say it's farther away
- Status: ✅ (textbook illusion; NASA calls a Ponzo-type explanation the most popular)
- Sources: [T1] NASA Ep. 50 · [T1] Ross & Plug (2002).

## C18: Supermoons: at perigee the moon can be up to ~14% bigger and ~30% brighter than at its farthest
