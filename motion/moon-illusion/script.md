## CHAPTER 1: THE SAME MOON

DOC: Let's start with what's actually up there. [op]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | Doc points | night sky with moon high and low
DOC: The moon is about three hundred and eighty four thousand kilometres away, and about three and a half thousand kilometres wide. [C23]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | Earth–moon scale bar with numbers
DOC: And it's slowly leaving. Mirrors left on the moon by the Apollo astronauts let us bounce lasers off it, and they show it drifting away by almost four centimetres a year. [C23]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | laser beam Earth→moon reflector, "+4 cm / year" ticker
CELL: Honestly? Relatable. Sometimes I need space too. [op]
DOC: Four centimetres a year is not going to change what you see this weekend. [op]
DOC: From down here, that makes it look tiny. It covers about half a degree of sky, and that number barely changes, whether the moon is rising or right overhead. [C1]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | protractor arc, 0.5° wedge, same wedge at two heights
DOC: You can test it. Hold something about the size of a pea at arm's length. It covers the rising moon, and hours later, it still covers the high moon. [C17]
  > SCENE: Rooftop | avatar holds a pea at arm's length, pea eclipses moon low, then high
CELL: I tried that. Now I have a pea and no friends. [op]
DOC: Photos agree. Line up pictures taken hours apart, and the moon's disc is the same size. [C1]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | filmstrip of photos, discs line up
DOC: In fact, the rising moon is slightly farther from you. Overhead, you're closer by about half the Earth's width, the distance from the surface to the centre, so on the horizon it's about one and a half percent farther away. [C2]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | Earth diagram, observer on the surface; overhead line is shorter by surface-to-centre (one radius)
DOC: And no, the air isn't a magnifying glass. If anything, it squashes the low moon a little flatter. [C4]
  > SCENE: LabBench | Doc holds a magnifier, crosses it out; moon squashes slightly
DOC: One thing is real, though: the colour. Low moonlight crosses much more air, which scatters away blue light and leaves the orange. Same reason sunsets turn red. [C5]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | light ray through thick vs thin atmosphere band, blue dots bounce away
DOC: So the light is genuinely different. The size is not. [op]

## CHAPTER 2: TWO THOUSAND YEARS OF ARGUING

DOC: People have noticed this for a very long time. [C6]
  > SCENE: Timeline | scrolls, oil lamps, ancient sky
DOC: Aristotle wrote about it more than two thousand three hundred years ago. [C6]
  > SCENE: Avatar in a toga (scholar look) under a giant moon, timeline tag "4th CENTURY BC"
DOC: In his big astronomy book, Ptolemy blamed the air. He thought the atmosphere was enlarging the moon. [C6]
  > SCENE: Scholar 2 with an astrolabe, air drawn as a lens, then a red X
DOC: A Greek astronomer named Cleomedes, about eighteen hundred years ago, suggested it might also be about distance: hazy air makes the low moon seem farther away. [C6]
  > SCENE: Scholar with a scroll, tag "~200 AD", thought bubble with a far-away moon
CELL: To be fair, I also blame the air. For most things. [op]
DOC: About a thousand years ago, the scholar Ibn al-Haytham made a sharper argument. He said it's about how far away the moon seems. [C6]
  > SCENE: Scholar 3 at a desk with a book of optics, diagram glowing
DOC: He wrote huge books on how vision works, and he still kept part of the old air explanation too. Nobody gets everything right on the first try. [C6]
  > SCENE: Stack of manuscripts, light-ray diagrams
DOC: When the moon is low, you see it beyond fields, trees, hills, houses. All that stuff in between makes the horizon feel far away. [C7]
  > SCENE: Landscape (Farm → Mountains) with dotted distance chain from viewer to horizon moon
DOC: Overhead, there's nothing in between. Just empty sky. So your brain treats the sky like a flattened dome: far at the edges, close at the top. [C7]
  > SCENE: Diagram | flattened dome over the viewer, moon riding the dome
DOC: And if two things make the same-sized image in your eye, but one seems farther away, your brain decides the far one must be bigger. [C12]
  > SCENE: Ponzo illusion: two identical bars on railway tracks, far one looks bigger, then measured equal
DOC: It's the same trick as this classic illusion. These two bars are exactly the same length. [C12]
  > SCENE: Ponzo close-up, ruler slides across both

## CHAPTER 3: YOUR BRAIN IS A BAD RULER

DOC: In 1962, two psychologists, Lloyd Kaufman and Irvin Rock, put this to the test. [C9]
  > SCENE: LabWide | 1962 tag | two avatars with a contraption of mirrors on a tripod
DOC: They tried changing the angle of the viewer's eyes. That didn't matter much. [C9]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | three switches flip, needle barely moves
DOC: But when they blocked the view of the landscape, the giant moon shrank back down. [C9]
  > SCENE: Landscape slides away behind a card; moon deflates
DOC: In 2000, Kaufman and his son James built artificial moons that floated in the real sky, using an optical device. [C10]
  > SCENE: LabWide | 2000 tag | father and son avatars, stereo viewer, two glowing moons
DOC: People set the horizon moon about four times farther away than the high one. [C10]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | two markers on a distance line, "≈4×"
DOC: But here's the weird part. Ask people directly, and most say the giant moon looks closer. [C11]
  > SCENE: Avatars with speech bubbles "CLOSER!", Doc shrugs
CELL: So my brain thinks it's far, and also near. That explains my driving. [op]
DOC: Really, man? [op]
DOC: Scientists call it a paradox. One idea is that your brain first decides "far away, so big", and then "big, so close". The two judgements fight. [C10]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | brain diagram with two arrows looping, FAR → BIG → CLOSE
DOC: There are other suspects too. Some say the moon just looks big next to tiny distant trees and houses. [C13]
  > SCENE: Moon next to tiny trees vs moon alone in a huge empty sky
DOC: But sailors still see the giant moon rising over an empty ocean, with nothing to compare it to. [C14]
  > SCENE: Beach / open sea, moon rising over a flat horizon, a little sailboat
DOC: Other researchers think the way your eyes focus and point plays a part. [C8]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | two eyeballs converging, labelled "FOCUS?"
DOC: And here's a fun one. Bend over, look at the rising moon upside down between your legs, and for many people it shrinks. [C15]
  > SCENE: Rooftop | avatar bends over; inverted view, moon smaller
CELL: The neighbours are going to love this episode. [op]
DOC: It isn't just the moon, either. Constellations and the setting sun look bigger near the horizon too. [C16]
  > SCENE: Night sky with Orion low and big, then high and small; sunset sun
DOC: After all these centuries, there's still no single winning explanation. Even a NASA scientist, asked this exact question, says: we don't really know. [C8]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | card "WE DON'T REALLY KNOW" (no logo), Doc thinking

## CHAPTER 4: WHAT REALLY CHANGES

DOC: Now, the moon's size does change a little. Just not when it rises. [C18]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | elliptical orbit, moon near and far
DOC: Its orbit is slightly oval. When a full moon happens at the close end, we call it a supermoon. [C18]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | orbit diagram, perigee marker, "SUPERMOON"
DOC: At its biggest, a full moon can look up to about fourteen percent wider and thirty percent brighter than the smallest full moon of the year. [C18]
  > SCENE: Two moon discs side by side, measured, "+14%"
DOC: At the far end of the orbit, you get the opposite: a smaller, dimmer full moon some people call a micromoon. [C18]
  > SCENE: Same two discs, small one labelled "MICROMOON"
DOC: Which, honestly, most people can't tell by eye. Your brain's horizon trick adds far more than that, often a third to a half bigger. [C19, C3]
  > SCENE: Bars: SUPERMOON +14% vs YOUR BRAIN +50%, brain bar towers
CELL: So my brain is the real supermoon. [op]
DOC: …I'm going to let you have that one. [op]
DOC: The Hunter's Moon has its own trick, though. This time of year, the moon's path meets the evening horizon at a very shallow angle. [C21]
  > SCENE: LabScreen | horizon line with the moon's path at a steep angle (spring) vs shallow (autumn)
DOC: So for a few nights, where most of us live, it rises only about half an hour later each evening, instead of the usual fifty minutes. Bright moonlight, right after sunset, night after night. [C21]
  > SCENE: Farm at dusk, three nights, moon rising almost in the same spot
DOC: Before electric lights, that was a gift. The old story is that hunters used it to stock up before winter. That's where the name comes from. [C20]
  > SCENE: Farm/Forest at dusk, avatar with lantern, barn, autumn leaves
DOC: And after this one comes a run of supermoons: three or four in a row, depending on who's counting, starting with November's Beaver Moon. [C22]
  > SCENE: Calendar: NOV, DEC, JAN with moons; FEB faded with a "?"
