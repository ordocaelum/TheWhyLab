Now, the moon's size does change a little. Just not when it rises.

Its orbit is slightly oval. When a full moon happens at the close end, we call it a supermoon.

At its biggest, a full moon can look up to about fourteen percent wider and thirty percent brighter than the smallest full moon of the year.

At the far end of the orbit, you get the opposite: a smaller, dimmer full moon some people call a micromoon.

Which, honestly, most people can't tell by eye. Your brain's horizon trick adds far more than that, often a third to a half bigger.

The Hunter's Moon has its own trick, though. This time of year, the moon's path meets the evening horizon at a very shallow angle.

So for a few nights, where most of us live, it rises only about half an hour later each evening, instead of the usual fifty minutes. Bright moonlight, right after sunset, night after night.

Before electric lights, that was a gift. The old story is that hunters used it to stock up before winter. That's where the name comes from.

And after this one comes a run of supermoons: three or four in a row, depending on who's counting, starting with November's Beaver Moon.
