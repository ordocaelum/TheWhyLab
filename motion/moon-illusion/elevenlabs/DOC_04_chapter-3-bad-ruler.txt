In 1962, two psychologists, Lloyd Kaufman and Irvin Rock, put this to the test.

They tried changing the angle of the viewer's eyes. That didn't matter much.

But when they blocked the view of the landscape, the giant moon shrank back down.

In 2000, Kaufman and his son James built artificial moons that floated in the real sky, using an optical device.

People set the horizon moon about four times farther away than the high one.

But here's the weird part. Ask people directly, and most say the giant moon looks closer.

Really, man?

Scientists call it a paradox. One idea is that your brain first decides "far away, so big", and then "big, so close". The two judgements fight.

There are other suspects too. Some say the moon just looks big next to tiny distant trees and houses.

But sailors still see the giant moon rising over an empty ocean, with nothing to compare it to.

Other researchers think the way your eyes focus and point plays a part.

And here's a fun one. Bend over, look at the rising moon upside down between your legs, and for many people it shrinks.

It isn't just the moon, either. Constellations and the setting sun look bigger near the horizon too.

After all these centuries, there's still no single winning explanation. Even a NASA scientist, asked this exact question, says: we don't really know.
