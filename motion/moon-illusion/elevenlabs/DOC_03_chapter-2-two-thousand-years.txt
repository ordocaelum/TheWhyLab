People have noticed this for a very long time.

Aristotle wrote about it more than two thousand three hundred years ago.

In his big astronomy book, Ptolemy blamed the air. He thought the atmosphere was enlarging the moon.

A Greek astronomer named Cleomedes, about eighteen hundred years ago, suggested it might also be about distance: hazy air makes the low moon seem farther away.

About a thousand years ago, the scholar Ibn al-Haytham made a sharper argument. He said it's about how far away the moon seems.

He wrote huge books on how vision works, and he still kept part of the old air explanation too. Nobody gets everything right on the first try.

When the moon is low, you see it beyond fields, trees, hills, houses. All that stuff in between makes the horizon feel far away.

Overhead, there's nothing in between. Just empty sky. So your brain treats the sky like a flattened dome: far at the edges, close at the top.

And if two things make the same-sized image in your eye, but one seems farther away, your brain decides the far one must be bigger.

It's the same trick as this classic illusion. These two bars are exactly the same length.
