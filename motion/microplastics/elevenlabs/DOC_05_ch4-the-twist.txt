Here's how many of these studies measured plastic. They heat a tissue sample until it breaks apart, then read the chemical fragments, like a fingerprint.

The problem? Some fragments from ordinary body fat look a lot like fragments from plastic.

Take out the water, and the brain is about sixty percent fat.

In other words, some of that plastic... might not be plastic at all.

One analysis listed eighteen studies that didn't properly account for these false positives. Scientists have now formally challenged at least seven high-profile papers, including the brain study, the artery study and the bottled water study.

Some critics even suggest that rising obesity, more fat in the body, could explain part of the jump in brain plastic.

The original researchers push back. They say the critics are speculating without data of their own, though they admit their methods can still be improved.

One of the scientists behind the blood study says she's still convinced the plastic is there. But the true amount could be half what they measured, or ten times more.

Critics say quite a few papers, in the rush to publish, skipped basic lab checks, like running blank samples to catch contamination.

So is the whole microplastics scare just hype? Not so fast.
